**How attachment style shapes fear of rejection and friendship formation among BRAC University undergraduates**  
PSY 101 · Summer 2026 · Group 02 · Section 11 · Ms. Safina Binte Enayet · BRAC University

The slot is **ten minutes**. This script is **9 minutes 23 seconds** of actual speech, written at a normal presenting pace of about 145 words a minute. The thirty-seven seconds left over are for the walk-ups and the ten-second silence on slide 02. They are not for extra sentences.

The slides carry almost no text on purpose. The words are here. Press **S** during the presentation to get this same script on screen with a live countdown, and **T** to start the timer.

Presentation marking is **individual**, so read your own section until you own it. You do not need to memorise it. Memorise your first sentence and your last sentence; improvise the middle from the numbers.

## Questions you should expect

**“What is actually new here?” — the one to be ready for.**  
Three things. The data is ours: 103 students, our own survey, our own focus group, nothing borrowed. The instrument is new: we split anxiety and expected acceptance into two separate ratings, where the standard measure multiplies them together and never reports whether they are related. And the comparison is new: a prototype paragraph against dimensional scales, in the same students on the same afternoon, cross-validated. The literature settled that argument theoretically. We could not find anyone who ran the predictive test.

**“Is the paragraph winning just because it uses more parameters?”**  
Three dummy variables against two continuous predictors, so barely more. And cross-validation penalises extra parameters, which is exactly why we used it. The paragraph still wins on students the model has never seen, and the scales go below zero.

**“Is picking a paragraph reliable?”**  
Across weeks, that is a known limitation and we list it. Within our session it was consistent: 84 of 103 students, 81.6 per cent, chose the paragraph they had independently rated highest, against chance of p below .001. They are not answering randomly. They are answering a different question from the one the scales ask.

**“Half your sample is from one department.”**  
Correct, 53 of 103 from CSE, and it is our first stated limitation. It limits how far we generalise. It does not affect the internal comparison, because every student gave us both the paragraph and the scales.

**“Only nine people in the focus group?”**  
For thematic claims, yes, and we do not generalise from nine people. The statistical claim compares question types against each other across 71 coded turns inside that session. The unit is the turn, not the person.

**“Why is the video blurred and silent?”**  
Our consent form covered audio recording and anonymous quotation. It did not cover showing faces or playing voices, so we blurred and muted it.

**“Did family background predict anything?”**  
No, and we report that null. With 83 of 103 raised by both parents, our retrospective measures were too coarse to carry signal. That is a limitation of our instrument, not evidence against the developmental account.

**“Six dots is not much to draw a line through.”**  
Agreed, and we report it as a description of the six situations rather than a strong claim. The point is the contrast: the same two variables give a strong relationship across situations and effectively nothing across students.

**“Isn't this just introversion?”**  
Introversion would predict fewer conversations across the board. What we see is specific to rejection: the same students are almost fearless about asking for academic help and expensive about suggesting lunch. The cost tracks the social risk, not the amount of contact.

**“What would you do differently?”**  
Recruit the follow-up sample before the survey rather than after it, and ask the focus group indirect questions from the start. Our sharpest questions produced our thinnest answers, and we only discovered that after coding the transcript.

## What was stripped out of the wording, and why

1. **The “not X, but Y” flip** — “It isn't a literature review, it's our own data.” Generated text loves the negation-then-correction shape because it manufactures contrast for free. Replaced with a plain positive claim: “Everything on this slide is ours.”

2. **Em-dash as a rhetorical hinge** — Three or four em-dashes in one paragraph is the loudest tell there is. Almost all of them are now full stops. Short sentences sound like confidence out loud; dashes sound like an essay being read.

3. **Stacked triads** — “The motivation and the feeling and the behaviour.” Rule-of-three padding. Kept once, on slide 13, where the three things are genuinely three things.

4. **Hedged sincerity openers** — “I want to be clear that…”, “Let me be honest…”, “To be fair…”. These announce sincerity instead of being sincere. Deleted. Mahir keeps one “now an honest confession”, because an actual confession follows it.

5. **Signposting boilerplate** — “Today we will be presenting…”, “Now let's move on to…”, “As you can see on this slide…”, “In conclusion…”, “That concludes our presentation.” All removed. The talk starts inside a story and ends on “thank you”.

6. **Connective filler** — “That matters, because…”, “Here's the thing…”, “Here's what happened…”, “It is worth noting that…”, “This is where X comes in.” Cut, or replaced by the claim itself.

7. **Corporate vocabulary** — delve, leverage, robust, underscore, utilise, moreover, furthermore, crucially, notably, holistic, key takeaway, deep dive, unpack, resonate, journey, landscape, foster, navigate. None of these appear anywhere in the script.

8. **Hollow superlatives** — fascinating, remarkable, profound, incredibly, truly, significant used loosely. Removed. The numbers carry the weight, and adjectives stacked on top of them sound like padding.

9. **Perfect symmetry, repeated** — Antithesis pairs like “Situations line up. Students don't.” work, but become a giveaway when used four times. Kept once, in Abdullah's Result 2, and nowhere else.

10. **Uniform sentence rhythm** — Generated prose runs every sentence to a similar length. This script alternates on purpose: “Gone. All six bars sit on zero.” sits next to a long explanatory sentence.

11. **Slide bullets read aloud** — “New data: 103 students. New instrument: six situations.” That is slide text, not speech. Rewritten into sentences a person would actually say to another person.

12. **Emphasis on everything** — The earlier draft italicised a phrase every few lines. Emphasis that constant is no emphasis. Bold is now reserved for stage directions, and the delivery notes say where to slow down instead.

13. **Apologising for null results** — “Unfortunately, two of our hypotheses were not supported.” Rewritten as Maysha's line: “I am not apologising for that. We predicted the ordinary thing and the data handed us a better one.”

14. **Generic closer** — “Thank you for your attention, we would be happy to take any questions you may have.” Replaced with a callback to Rafi and a two-word stop.

15. **Explaining the joke** — The old draft followed its best quote with a sentence explaining what the quote meant. Both places now end on the quote and a pause.

## How to rehearse it

- Run it twice with a stopwatch before the day. The number that matters is your own slice, not the total.
- Rehearse two beats together, out loud, at least three times: Azmyeen's ten-second silence on slide 02, and Abdullah's callback on slide 09. The opening is wasted if either one is rushed.
- One person drives the laptop for all six speakers. Passing a laptop mid-talk costs about eight seconds each time, and there are five handoffs.
- Learn your first sentence and your last sentence by heart. Improvise the middle from the numbers. Nobody notices a paraphrase; everybody notices a stumble in the first line.
- Say the next speaker's name as your last word. It is the cleanest handoff there is and it prevents two people starting at once.
- Do not read the slide. Every number on screen is legible from the back row. Your job is the sentence that is not on screen.
- When a question comes, answer it and then stop. The answers in this script are one paragraph each on purpose.
- Monjurul carries 2:06 and Maysha 1:14. If you want them level, hand slide 12 (the models) to Maysha — it tests the hypotheses she introduced, so it reads naturally — and update the speaker map in app.js before you rebuild.
