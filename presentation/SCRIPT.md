### 03 · Rationale

> We picked this because three of us watched a friend go quiet across a semester, and none of us had a word for it beyond shy.
>
> Bowlby's idea is that what you learn about closeness at home becomes a running assumption about whether people will show up for you. That assumption doesn't stay at home. It shows up in who types a message and deletes it.
>
> And here it's sharper: open credit dissolves the cohort every semester, so friendships have to be rebuilt again and again. *Sixteen per cent of the students we surveyed had no close friend at all.* Seventy-one per cent wouldn't talk to us in person.
>

## Tasfia Hasan — Novelty & Objectives · 1:15–2:27

### 04 · What is new here ← **the novelty slide**

> I want to be clear about what this project is, because it isn't a literature review.
>
> The data is *ours*. We built the survey, we ran it, 103 students answered it in three days in August, and we ran the focus group ourselves.
>
> The instrument is new. We took six real situations and asked about each one twice — how anxious would you feel, and how well do you think it would go. The standard measure multiplies those two together and never checks whether they're related. Separating them is what let us test our third prediction at all.
>
> And the comparison is new. A paragraph you point at, against a rating scale, in the same students on the same afternoon. The field settled this by argument. *We could not find a study that actually ran it.*

**Cue:** This is your strongest slide. Say the last sentence slowly. Expect a question on it — the answer is in the Q&A section below.

### 05 · Objectives

> Three objectives.
>
> First, to describe how students of each style *talk* about approaching people and being turned down — not how they score, how they speak. That's why we used both a survey and a discussion.
>
> Second, to test whether the fears sitting between someone's style and their actual behaviour are the ones they report.
>
> Third, to compare the three insecure patterns against each other, instead of lumping them into one group the way most studies do.
>
> So we measured attachment style, fear of judgement and loneliness — and against those, real behaviour: how many close friends, how many conversations actually started, which situations get avoided.
>
> Maysha.

## K.M Mahir Labib — Methodology · 3:17–4:32

### 07 · Methodology

> We asked the same question twice — once with numbers, once with talk.
>
> 104 responses in August. We threw one out where every answer was impossible, including an age of a hundred million. That leaves 103.
>
> One honest change of plan. We'd promised sixteen interviews and three focus groups. We ran one group and added a survey, because 73 of our 103 respondents refused any follow-up contact. *That refusal stopped being a problem and became a finding.*
>
> Everyone read four paragraphs and picked one, then answered rating scales. And the six situations were each rated twice — that double rating is what makes our second result possible.
>
> In the room we never said the words anxious or avoidant. Label someone and they perform the label. Consent was written, names became pseudonyms, and everyone left with the counselling centre's card.

## Md. Monjurul Hasan Bhuiyan — Result 3, Model, Close · 6:07–7:42

### 11 · Result 3 — the room would not say it

> Our third result is the one we didn't expect, and it's about our own questions.
>
> We wrote questions designed to pull out a personal story — who's been Rafi this semester, tell us about a time. Those got a real personal answer *three times out of forty-five*.
>
> The questions that asked for nothing like that got one nine times out of twenty-six. Five times the rate.
>
> The harder we asked, the less people told us. And it wasn't one or two shy people — it didn't vary by speaker. It's the room.
>
> Look at where the answers went. Only seventeen per cent were something that actually happened to them. Nearly half were about people in general.
>
> The clearest thing anyone said in sixty minutes was four words long — and it was about the character: *"I have been Rafi."*

**Cue:** Pause after the quote. Don't explain it.

### 12 · The models

> Everything you have seen came out of a model, so here they are.
>
> Top left is the comparison itself. The four groups, and how many close friends each one actually has. Secure students hold four and a half. Fearful students hold under two.
>
> Beside it is every test we ran and the question it answers. A MANOVA to ask whether the four groups differ at all. Games and Howell to ask which ones. A cross-validated regression to ask paragraph or scales. Fisher's exact for the disclosure gap. And k-means, to check whether the scales find the same four groups the students picked. *They do not, and that is the point.*
>
> Underneath, two of those are running live. On the left we hide one student and guess them from the other 102, over and over. On the right we reshuffle who opened up, a thousand times, to see whether chance could produce our result. About six times in a thousand, it can.

**Cue:** Point, don't read. The k-means line is the strongest one here: the scales do not recover the groups students recognise in themselves.

### 13 · Discussion

> So why does a paragraph beat a scale?
>
> Because people judge themselves against a whole picture, not a checklist. A paragraph holds the motivation and the feeling and the behaviour together — six separate questions pull them apart and lose something.
>
> And the scales missed because people who avoid closeness often don't know they do it. They under-rate themselves on exactly the questions meant to catch them.
>
> Our limits, honestly: half our sample was from one department, more men than women, one focus group of nine, one moment in time — so we can't claim causes — and our tests can't rule out small effects.
>
> What I learned: we thought a well-built question extracts an answer. *The opposite happened.* And we assumed our job was to find the anxious students. The ones who worried us by the end reported the least distress and the fewest friends.

### 14 · Close

> To close.
>
> Among these 103 students, the paragraph someone recognised themselves in predicted their friendships. Two validated scales did not.
>
> So: offer people *recognition rather than assessment* — descriptions to see themselves in, not scales to score themselves on. And give students an academic reason to talk, because asking for help with coursework was the cheapest social act we measured and suggesting lunch was the most expensive.
>
> Study partners, help desks, rotating seating. Students will take an academic reason to talk long before a social one.
>
> Thank you — happy to take questions.

**Cue:** Stop. Add nothing after "questions".

## Likely questions

**"What's actually new here?"** — the one to be ready for.
Three things. The data is ours: 103 students, our own survey, our own focus group. The instrument is new: we split anxiety and expected acceptance into two separate ratings, where the standard measure multiplies them together and never reports whether they're related. And the comparison is new: a prototype paragraph against dimensional scales, in the same students on the same afternoon, cross-validated. The literature settled that question by argument about latent structure — we could not find a study that ran the predictive test.

**"Isn't the paragraph winning just because it uses more parameters?"**
Three dummy variables against two continuous predictors — barely more. And cross-validation penalises extra parameters, which is exactly why we used it. The paragraph still wins on students the model has never seen; the scales go below zero.

**"Isn't picking a paragraph unreliable?"**
Across weeks, yes — that's a known limitation and we list it. But within our session it was consistent: 81.6% chose the paragraph they had independently rated highest, against chance p < .001. They're not answering randomly. They're answering a different question from the one the scales ask.

**"Half your sample is from one department."**
Correct, and it's our first limitation. It limits how far we can generalise. It doesn't affect the internal comparison, because every student gave us both the paragraph and the scales.

**"Only nine people in the focus group?"**
For thematic claims, yes, and we don't generalise from it. The statistical claim compares question *types* against each other across 71 coded turns inside that session — the unit is the turn, not the person.

**"Why is the video blurred?"**
Our consent form covered audio recording and anonymous quotation. It did not cover showing faces. So the footage is blurred and silent.

**"Did family background predict anything?"**
No, and we report that null. With 83 of 103 raised by both parents, our retrospective measures were too coarse to carry signal — a limitation of our instrument, not evidence against the developmental account.

**"Six dots isn't much to draw a line through."**
Agreed, and we report it as a description of the situations, not a strong claim. The point is the contrast: the same two variables give a strong relationship across situations and effectively nothing across students.
